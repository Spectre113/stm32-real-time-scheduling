\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system_model}

We model the workload by a set of periodic tasks $\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_n\}$ executed on a uniprocessor. We consider only implicit deadlines, i.e, $D_i=T_i$. Each task $\tau_i=(C_i,T_i=D_i)$ generates a sequence of jobs $J_i^j$, where $j$ denotes the scheduled release index. Consecutive scheduled releases satisfy $R_i^{j+1}=R_i^j+T_i$.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{System model notation.}
  \label{tab:system_model}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
  \begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
    \hline
    \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
    \hline
    $\mathcal{T}, n$ & Task set and number of tasks \\
    $\tau_i$ & Task $i$ \\
    $C_i$ & Execution time of a job of $\tau_i$ \\
    $T_i$ & Task period \\
    $D_i=T_i$ & Relative deadline, equal to the period \\
    $J_i^j$ & Job associated with the $j$-th scheduled release of $\tau_i$ \\
    $R_i^j$ & Scheduled release time \\
    $f_i^j$ & Completion time of an executed job \\
    $d_i^j=R_i^j+D_i$ & Absolute deadline \\
    $U=\sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i/T_i$ & Task-set utilization \\
    \hline
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:system_model} summarizes the notation. Release indices include skipped activations, for which no completion time is defined.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A schedule specifies which job executes on the processor at each time instant, or whether the processor executes no job.
  \item Preemption interrupts a job before completion to execute another job. The interrupted job may resume later.
  \item A completed job $J_i^j$ has missed its deadline if $f_i^j>d_i^j$.
  \item The response time of a completed job $J_i^j$ is $f_i^j-R_i^j$, the time from its scheduled release to completion.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scheduling Principles}
\label{sec:sched_principles}

\subsection{Super Loop scheduler}
The considered Super Loop scheduler repeatedly scans tasks in a fixed order and executes each selected job non-preemptively. To illustrate its behavior, let us consider the two periodic tasks in Table~\ref{tab:superloop_tasks}. Both tasks first release at $t=0$.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Task set used in the Super Loop example.}
  \label{tab:superloop_tasks}
  \begin{tabular}{c|ccc}
    \hline
    \textbf{Task} & $C_i$ & $T_i$ & $D_i$ \\
    \hline
    $\tau_1$ & 20 & 50 & 50 \\
    $\tau_2$ & 10 & 20 & 20 \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The resulting Super Loop schedule is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:superloop_schedule}.

\begin{figure*}[tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{superloop_execution_schedule.png}
  \caption{Super Loop execution scenario for the task set in Table~\ref{tab:superloop_tasks}.}
  \label{fig:superloop_schedule}
\end{figure*}

Let $t^{cur}$ denote the current system time and $t_i^{next}$ the next scheduled release time of task $\tau_i$. During execution of $J_i^j$, the release variable $t_i^{next}$ retains $R_i^j$. At completion, $f_i^j$ denotes the completion time, and a deadline miss is recorded if $f_i^j>d_i^j$. The scheduler then advances $t_i^{next}$ by $T_i$. While $t_i^{next}<f_i^j$, the scheduled release at $t_i^{next}$ is counted as skipped and $t_i^{next}$ is advanced by $T_i$ again. A release at $t_i^{next}=f_i^j$ is retained.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:superloop} summarizes the illustrated scheduling logic. Since $R_i^1=0$, the release index is $j=1+t_i^{next}/T_i$, including skipped releases. The clock used by \textsc{Now} starts at zero.

The corresponding scheduling state is shown in Table~\ref{tab:superloop_state}.

In Fig.~\ref{fig:superloop_schedule}, $J_1^1$ executes from $t=0$ to $t=20$. Then, $J_2^1$ executes until $t=30$, missing its deadline at $t=20$. At completion, the release scheduled for $R_2^2=20$ is counted as skipped and $t_2^{next}$ advances to $R_2^3=40$. Thus, no $J_2^2$ is executed.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
	\caption{Super Loop Scheduler}
	\label{alg:superloop}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]

		\Function{SuperLoop}{$\mathcal{T}$}

		\ForAll{$\tau_i \in \mathcal{T}$}
		\State $t_i^{next} \gets 0$
		\EndFor

		\While{true}

		\For{$i \gets 1$ to $n$}
		\Comment{Fixed task order}

		\State $t^{cur} \gets \Call{Now}{}$

		\If{$t^{cur} \geq t_i^{next}$}

		\State $j \gets 1+t_i^{next}/T_i$
		\State $R_i^j \gets t_i^{next}$
		\State \Call{Execute}{$J_i^j$}
		\Comment{Non-preemptive}

		\State $f_i^j \gets \Call{Now}{}$

		\If{$f_i^j > R_i^j + D_i$}
		\State \Call{RecordMiss}{$J_i^j$}
		\EndIf

		\State $t_i^{next} \gets t_i^{next} + T_i$

		\While{$t_i^{next} < f_i^j$}
		\State \Call{RecordSkip}{$\tau_i, t_i^{next}$}
		\State $t_i^{next} \gets t_i^{next} + T_i$
		\EndWhile

		\EndIf

		\EndFor

		\EndWhile

		\EndFunction

	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Earliest Deadline First}
\label{EDF_subsection}
Unlike Super Loop, Earliest Deadline First (EDF) selects jobs according
to their absolute deadlines.

We first consider preemptive EDF with immediate preemption. At each scheduling decision, the ready job with the earliest absolute deadline $d_i^j$ is selected (Section~\ref{sec:system_model}). If a newly released job has an earlier absolute deadline than the running job, it preempts the running job.

Fig.~\ref{fig:edf_schedule} illustrates EDF scheduling for the task set in Table~\ref{tab:superloop_tasks}.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{edf_execution_schedule.png}
	\caption{EDF execution scenario for the task set in
		Table~\ref{tab:superloop_tasks}. Hatching indicates a preempted
		job awaiting resumption.}
	\label{fig:edf_schedule}
\end{figure*}

At $t=20$, $J_2^2$ is released with $d_2^2=40<d_1^1=50$ and preempts $J_1^1$. After $J_2^2$ completes at $t=30$, $J_1^1$ resumes and completes at $t=40$.

Chunked EDF is used as a simplified prototype implementation, avoiding the additional complexity of full task-context switching during initial evaluation. Synthetic jobs execute in chunks of maximum duration $\Delta$, with job selection repeated after each chunk. An earlier-deadline job released during a chunk may therefore wait until the chunk completes. Sensor jobs use separate staged handlers.

For illustration, Fig.~\ref{fig:chunked_edf} uses $\Delta=4$ ms. Job $J_2^2$ is released at $t=20$ ms and preempts $J_1^1$ at the next chunk boundary $t=22$ ms. Thus, the preemption delay is $\delta=2$ ms. The final chunk of $J_2^2$ lasts $2$ ms, since the preceding two chunks account for $8$ ms of its execution time $C_2=10$ ms.

\begin{figure*}[!b]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{chunked_edf_execution_schedule.png}
	\caption{Chunk-bounded preemption in the EDF implementation.}
	\label{fig:chunked_edf}
\end{figure*}

Ignoring scheduling overhead, the delay until the next chunk boundary satisfies $0 \leq \delta \leq \Delta$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:chunked_edf} summarizes chunked EDF for synthetic jobs, assuming first releases at $t=0$ as in the example. The set $\mathcal{R}$ contains jobs admitted for execution but not yet completed. Initially, $\mathcal{R}=\emptyset$, $t_i^{next}=0$, and no job is active. Equal deadlines are resolved by task order. \textsc{ExecuteChunk} executes at most $\Delta$ of the selected job's remaining execution and preserves its progress.

\textsc{ReleaseJob} creates the job associated with $t_i^{next}$, sets its release and absolute deadline, and adds it to $\mathcal{R}$ as active. \textsc{CompleteJob} checks its deadline and advances $t_i^{next}$ using the skip rule in Algorithm~\ref{alg:superloop}, then removes the job from $\mathcal{R}$ and marks the task inactive.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
	\caption{Chunked EDF Scheduler}
	\label{alg:chunked_edf}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]

		\Function{ChunkedEDF}{$\mathcal{T},\Delta$}

		\While{true}

		\State $t^{cur} \gets \Call{Now}{}$

		\ForAll{$\tau_i \in \mathcal{T}$}
		\If{$t_i^{next} \leq t^{cur}$ and $\tau_i$ has no active job}
		\State \Call{ReleaseJob}{$\tau_i,t_i^{next}$}
		\EndIf
		\EndFor

		\If{$\mathcal{R} \neq \emptyset$}

		\State $J_i^j \gets
		\Call{SelectEDF}{\mathcal{R}}$

		\State \Call{ExecuteChunk}{$J_i^j,\Delta$}

		\If{\Call{Completed}{$J_i^j$}}
		\State $f_i^j \gets \Call{Now}{}$
		\State \Call{CompleteJob}{$J_i^j,f_i^j$}
		\EndIf

		\EndIf

		\EndWhile

		\EndFunction
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\subsection{Experimental setup}
Experiments run on the NUCLEO-F767ZI at 96 MHz. Physical sensors are disabled in these experiments. Each synthetic job executes a timed busy-wait workload, the scheduler also uses busy polling. Timing is obtained from the DWT cycle counter, and UART reports are emitted after measurement stops.

The results below use three tasks with $(T_1,T_2,T_3)=(10,50,200)$ ms and $D_i=T_i$. Table~\ref{tab:experimental_workloads} lists the configured execution budgets. Thus, $U$ denotes nominal workload utilization and excludes instrumentation and scheduling overhead. Both schedulers use the same task set, Super Loop scans tasks in index order. For clarity, the illustrative schedules assume synchronous first releases at $t=0$, whereas the implementation releases each task for the first time one period after initialization, $R_i^1=t_0+T_i$.

The main comparison uses 60 s windows and $\Delta=1$ ms for EDF. The chunk-size study uses $\Delta\in\{1,2,4\}$ ms at $U\in\{50,90,100\}\%$, the figure below shows $U=90\%$. Each configuration is run three times from reset. Additional campaigns use two tasks and 100 s windows to examine task-set and window-length effects. Extended diagnostic statistics are disabled in all these campaigns.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
Let $N_i$, $M_i$, and $S_i$ be the numbers of completed jobs, completed jobs that missed their deadlines, and skipped releases of $\tau_i$ in a run. Let $W$ be the requested measurement-window duration. We report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Deadline-miss ratio: $100M_i/N_i$ percent, defined for $N_i>0$.
  \item Skipped-release ratio: $100S_i/(W/T_i)$ percent, normalized by the nominal number of releases in the requested window.
  \item Average response time: the mean of $f_i^j-R_i^j$ over completed jobs. The maximum observed response time is also recorded.
  \item Selection overhead: measured time spent selecting jobs in iterations that execute work, divided by the actual measurement duration, in percent. This excludes uninstrumented scheduler work and is not total processor utilization.
\end{itemize}

Measurement stops at the first loop boundary after $W$, its actual duration may therefore be slightly longer. Released but unfinished tracked jobs and their overdue status are recorded separately and excluded from completion-based metrics. Subsequent releases awaiting skip accounting are not included in this pending-job count. Plot points are arithmetic means of the corresponding per-run metrics over three runs, not pooled averages over all jobs.

\subsection{Super Loop and EDF comparison}
Fig.~\ref{fig:scheduler_comparison_tau1} compares the schedulers for $\tau_1$. Under Super Loop, its deadline-miss and skipped-release ratios generally increase with utilization, as does its average response time. With chunked EDF ($\Delta=1$ ms), no deadline misses or skipped releases are recorded for $\tau_1$ in the evaluated three-task configurations. Selecting jobs by absolute deadline reduces the blocking caused by the fixed Super Loop order.

Fig.~\ref{fig:scheduler_comparison_tau3} shows the corresponding results for $\tau_3$. Its average response time under chunked EDF increases with utilization as jobs with earlier absolute deadlines execute first. At nominal $U=100\%$, no capacity is reserved for scheduling and execution overhead, deadline misses and skipped releases are observed for $\tau_3$. The lower response time of $\tau_3$ under Super Loop occurs alongside skipped releases of $\tau_1$, so it does not imply better service for the entire task set.

\subsection{Effect of chunk duration}
Fig.~\ref{fig:chunk_size_tradeoff} examines $\Delta$ at $U=90\%$. Larger chunks reduce the frequency of scheduling decisions. In these measurements, the reported selection overhead decreases, while the average response time of $\tau_1$ increases and that of $\tau_3$ decreases. Longer non-preemptive intervals delay $\tau_1$, the result for $\tau_3$ also reflects the changed execution order and fewer scheduling interruptions. The overhead measurements remain preliminary pending validation of the instrumentation.
