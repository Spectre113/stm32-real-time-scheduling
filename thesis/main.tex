\section{Introduction}

STM32 is a family of microcontrollers developed by STMicroelectronics for embedded applications. These devices integrate a processor, memory, and peripheral interfaces on a single chip~\cite{STM32F767Datasheet}.

STM32-based devices support applications such as environmental monitoring~\cite{STM32EnvironmentMonitoring} and equipment condition monitoring~\cite{STM32ConditionMonitoring}. In these applications maintaining responsiveness as the workload increases is a key challenge.

The paper examines how the choice of scheduling approach affects responsiveness and processing cost on STM32. We compare two approaches: SuperLoop~\cite{KeilSuperLoop} and Earliest Deadline First (EDF)~\cite{LiuLayland1973}.

\subsection{Contribution}
We compare periodic SuperLoop and chunked EDF on an STM32F767. We evaluate the effects of workload utilization and chunk duration on response time, deadline misses, skipped releases, and measured selection overhead. Our implementations for both schedulers are publicly available.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system_model}

We rely on a classical real-time periodic system model by Liu and Layland~\cite{LiuLayland1973}. We adopt the task and release notation of Burmyakov and Bini~\cite{BurmyakovBini2026}.

A real-time system $\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_n\}$ consists of $n$ periodic tasks executed on a uniprocessor. Each task $\tau_i=(C_i,D_i,P_i)$ has a worst-case execution time $C_i$, a relative deadline $D_i$, and a period $P_i$. We consider implicit deadlines, $D_i=P_i$. Task utilization is $U_i=\nicefrac{C_i}{P_i}$, and task-set utilization is $U_{\mathcal{T}}=\sum_{\tau_i\in\mathcal{T}}U_i$.

Let $J_{i,j}$ denote the $j$-th job of $\tau_i$, released at $r_{i,j}$. Unlike the sporadic model in the referenced paper, consecutive releases are separated by exactly $P_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:constr_releases}
  r_{i,j+1}=r_{i,j}+P_i.
\end{equation}
Job $J_{i,j}$ requires at most $C_i$ units of CPU time and has an absolute deadline $d_{i,j}=r_{i,j}+D_i$. Let $f_{i,j}$ denote its completion time, if it completes. Release indices include jobs skipped by the implementation. These jobs have no completion time.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A schedule specifies which job executes on the processor at each time instant, or whether the processor executes no job.
  \item Preemption interrupts a job before completion to execute another job. The interrupted job may resume later.
  \item A completed job $J_{i,j}$ has missed its deadline if $f_{i,j}>d_{i,j}$.
  \item The response time of a completed job $J_{i,j}$ is $f_{i,j}-r_{i,j}$, the time from its release to completion.
\end{itemize}

\section{Schedulers}
\label{sec:schedulers}
We consider two schedulers: SuperLoop and chunked EDF.

\subsection{SuperLoop}

The key steps of the SuperLoop scheduler are outlined in Algorithm~\ref{alg:superloop}. Fig.~\ref{fig:scheduler_schedules} compares schedules for a common task set.
%
\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\caption{SuperLoop Scheduler}
	\label{alg:superloop}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Function{SuperLoop}{$\mathcal{T}$}
		
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n$
		\Comment{Initial releases}
		
		\While{true}
		\For{$i \gets 1$ to $n$}
		\Comment{Fixed task order}
		\State $t^{\mathsf{cur}} \gets \Call{Now}{}$
		
		\If{$t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \leq t^{\mathsf{cur}}$}
		\State $J_{i,j} \gets$ \Call{ReleaseJob}{$\tau_i,t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$}
		\State \Call{Execute}{$J_{i,j}$}
		\Comment{Non-preemptive}
		\State $f_{i,j} \gets \Call{Now}{}$
		\Comment{Completion time}
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets t_i^{\mathsf{next}} + P_i$
		\While{$t_i^{\mathsf{next}} < f_{i,j}$}
		\Comment{Equality retained}
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets t_i^{\mathsf{next}} + P_i$
		\EndWhile
		\EndIf
		\EndFor
		\EndWhile
		\EndFunction
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Earliest Deadline First}
\label{EDF_subsection}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
	\caption{Chunked EDF Scheduler}
	\label{alg:chunked_edf}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]

		\Function{ChunkedEDF}{$\mathcal{T},\Delta$}
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n$
		\Comment{Initial releases}

		\While{true}

		\State $t^{\mathsf{cur}} \gets \Call{Now}{}$

		\ForAll{$\tau_i \in \mathcal{T}$}
		\Comment{Check releases}
		\If{$t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \leq t^{\mathsf{cur}}$ and $\tau_i$ has no active job}
		\State \Call{ReleaseJob}{$\tau_i,t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$}
		\Comment{Add to $\mathcal{R}$}
		\EndIf
		\EndFor

		\If{$\mathcal{R} \neq \emptyset$}
		\Comment{Pending work}

		\State $J_{i,j} \gets
		\Call{SelectEDF}{\mathcal{R}}$
		\Comment{Earliest deadline}

		\State \Call{ExecuteChunk}{$J_{i,j},\Delta$}
		\Comment{At most $\Delta$}

		\If{\Call{Completed}{$J_{i,j}$}}
		\State $f_{i,j} \gets \Call{Now}{}$
		\State \Call{CompleteJob}{$J_{i,j},f_{i,j}$}
		\Comment{Skip rule}
		\EndIf

		\EndIf

		\EndWhile

		\EndFunction
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\subsection{Experimental setup}
Experiments run on the NUCLEO-F767ZI at 96 MHz. Physical sensors are disabled in these experiments. Each synthetic job executes a timed busy-wait workload. The scheduler also uses busy polling. Timing is measured using the processor cycle counter in the Data Watchpoint and Trace (DWT) unit. Reports are transmitted over a serial interface (UART) after measurement stops.

The results below use three tasks with $(P_1,P_2,P_3)=(10,50,200)$ ms and $D_i=P_i$. Table~\ref{tab:experimental_workloads} lists the configured synthetic workload durations, which are nominal budgets rather than measured worst-case bounds. Thus, $U_{\mathcal{T}}$ denotes nominal workload utilization and excludes instrumentation and scheduling overhead. Both schedulers use the same task set. SuperLoop polls tasks in index order. The illustrative schedules assume synchronous first releases at $t=0$, whereas the implementation uses $r_{i,1}=t_0+P_i$, one period after initialization.

Extended diagnostic statistics are disabled in all these campaigns.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
Let $N_i$, $M_i$, and $S_i$ be the numbers of completed jobs, completed jobs that missed their deadlines, and skipped releases of $\tau_i$ in a run. Let $W$ be the requested measurement-window duration. We report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Deadline-miss ratio: $100M_i/N_i$ percent, defined for $N_i>0$.
  \item Skipped-release ratio: $100S_i/(W/P_i)$ percent, normalized by the nominal number of releases in the requested window.
  \item Average response time: the mean of $f_{i,j}-r_{i,j}$ over completed jobs. The maximum observed response time is also recorded.
  \item Selection overhead: measured time spent selecting jobs in iterations that execute work, divided by the actual measurement duration, in percent. This excludes uninstrumented scheduler work and is not total processor utilization.
\end{itemize}

Measurement stops at the first loop boundary after $W$, so its actual duration may be slightly longer. Released but unfinished tracked jobs and their overdue status are recorded separately and excluded from completion-based metrics. Subsequent releases awaiting skip accounting are not included in this pending-job count. Plot points are arithmetic means of the corresponding per-run metrics over three runs, not pooled averages over all jobs.

Fig.~\ref{fig:scheduler_comparison_tau1} shows the results for $\tau_1$. Under SuperLoop, its deadline-miss and skipped-release ratios generally increase with utilization, as does its average response time. In contrast, no deadline misses or skipped releases are observed under chunked EDF ($\Delta=1$ ms) for $\tau_1$ in the evaluated three-task configurations. EDF gives $\tau_1$ precedence over jobs with later absolute deadlines, whereas SuperLoop follows the fixed polling order.

Compared with SuperLoop, chunked EDF yields a higher average response time for $\tau_3$ in the evaluated configurations (Fig.~\ref{fig:scheduler_comparison_tau3}). Its response time increases with utilization. Under EDF, jobs with earlier absolute deadlines execute first. At nominal $U_{\mathcal{T}}=100\%$, no capacity is reserved for scheduling and execution overhead, and deadline misses and skipped releases are observed for $\tau_3$ under chunked EDF. However, the lower response time under SuperLoop occurs alongside skipped releases of $\tau_1$. It therefore does not imply better service for the entire task set.

\subsection{Effect of chunk duration}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]
	{chunk_size_tradeoff_u90_n3.pdf}
	\caption{Impact of chunk duration at $U_{\mathcal{T}}=90\%$ with three synthetic tasks and 60 s windows.}
	\label{fig:chunk_size_tradeoff}
\end{figure*}

Fig.~\ref{fig:chunk_size_tradeoff} shows the effects of increasing $\Delta$ at $U_{\mathcal{T}}=90\%$. Larger chunks reduce the frequency of scheduling decisions. The reported selection overhead decreases, whereas the average response time of $\tau_1$ increases and that of $\tau_3$ decreases. Longer non-preemptive chunks delay $\tau_1$. The decrease for $\tau_3$ may reflect changes in execution order and fewer scheduling interruptions. These overhead measurements remain preliminary pending validation of the instrumentation.
