\section{Introduction}
STM32 microcontrollers combine a processor, memory, and peripheral interfaces for embedded applications. The STM32F767 provides interfaces for acquiring sensor data, including analog inputs and a camera interface~\cite{STM32F767Datasheet}. Sensor acquisition and data processing share the processor, making their execution order relevant to timing requirements.

Sensor applications perform recurring tasks, such as reading temperature, measuring distance, capturing images, and acquiring audio. These tasks may differ in their periods and execution times. A long-running task can delay other tasks, even when their individual workloads are small. Scheduling must therefore account for when jobs become ready and when they must complete.

SuperLoop repeatedly calls software components within a main loop~\cite{KeilSuperLoop}. Our periodic implementation polls tasks in a fixed order and executes each selected job to completion. Earliest Deadline First (EDF) instead selects the ready job with the earliest absolute deadline~\cite{LiuLayland1973}. We implement chunked EDF, which repeats job selection at chunk boundaries. The chunk duration determines how long an earlier-deadline job may wait and how often the scheduler makes a selection.

\subsection{Contribution}
We compare periodic SuperLoop and chunked EDF on an STM32F767 using synthetic workloads. We evaluate the effects of workload utilization and chunk duration on response time, deadline misses, skipped releases, and measured selection overhead. Our implementations for both schedulers are publicly available.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system_model}

We rely on a classical real-time periodic system model by Liu and Layland~\cite{LiuLayland1973}. We adopt the task and release notation of Burmyakov and Bini~\cite{BurmyakovBini2026}.

A real-time system $\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_n\}$ consists of $n$ periodic tasks executed on a uniprocessor. Each task $\tau_i=(C_i,D_i,P_i)$ has a worst-case execution time $C_i$, a relative deadline $D_i$, and a period $P_i$. We consider implicit deadlines, $D_i=P_i$. Task utilization is $U_i=\nicefrac{C_i}{P_i}$, and task-set utilization is $U_{\mathcal{T}}=\sum_{\tau_i\in\mathcal{T}}U_i$.

Let $J_{i,j}$ denote the $j$-th job of $\tau_i$, released at $r_{i,j}$. Unlike the sporadic model in the referenced paper, consecutive releases are separated by exactly $P_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:constr_releases}
  r_{i,j+1}=r_{i,j}+P_i.
\end{equation}
Job $J_{i,j}$ requires at most $C_i$ units of CPU time and has an absolute deadline $r_{i,j}+D_i$. Its completion time, when executed to completion, is denoted by $f_{i,j}$. Release indices include jobs skipped by the implementation, these have no completion time.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A schedule specifies which job executes on the processor at each time instant, or whether the processor executes no job.
  \item Preemption interrupts a job before completion to execute another job. The interrupted job may resume later.
  \item A completed job $J_{i,j}$ has missed its deadline if $f_{i,j}>r_{i,j}+D_i$.
  \item The response time of a completed job $J_{i,j}$ is $f_{i,j}-r_{i,j}$, the time from its release to completion.
\end{itemize}

\section{Schedulers}
\label{sec:schedulers}
We describe the periodic SuperLoop and chunked EDF schedulers used in our evaluation.

\subsection{SuperLoop}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\caption{SuperLoop Scheduler}
	\label{alg:superloop}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Function{SuperLoop}{$\mathcal{T}$}
		
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n$
		\Comment{Initial releases}
		
		\While{true}
		\For{$i \gets 1$ to $n$}
		\Comment{Fixed task order}
		\State $t^{\mathsf{cur}} \gets \Call{Now}{}$
		
		\If{$t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \leq t^{\mathsf{cur}}$}
		\State $J_{i,j} \gets$ \Call{ReleaseJob}{$\tau_i,t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$}
		\State \Call{Execute}{$J_{i,j}$}
		\Comment{Non-preemptive}
		\State $f_{i,j} \gets \Call{Now}{}$
		\Comment{Completion time}
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets t_i^{\mathsf{next}} + P_i$
		\While{$t_i^{\mathsf{next}} < f_{i,j}$}
		\Comment{Equality retained}
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets t_i^{\mathsf{next}} + P_i$
		\EndWhile
		\EndIf
		\EndFor
		\EndWhile
		\EndFunction
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The considered SuperLoop implementation polls tasks in a fixed order and executes each ready job to completion. Algorithm~\ref{alg:superloop} outlines its key steps. To illustrate its behavior, we consider the two periodic tasks in Table~\ref{tab:superloop_tasks}. Both tasks first release at $t=0$.

Let $t^{\mathsf{cur}}$ denote the current system time and $t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$ the next release time tracked for task $\tau_i$. During execution of $J_{i,j}$, $t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$ retains $r_{i,j}$. At completion, the scheduler advances $t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$ by $P_i$. While $t_i^{\mathsf{next}}<f_{i,j}$, the corresponding release is skipped and $t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$ is advanced by $P_i$ again. A release at $t_i^{\mathsf{next}}=f_{i,j}$ is retained.

In Algorithm~\ref{alg:superloop}, \textsc{ReleaseJob} creates $J_{i,j}$ with $r_{i,j}=t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$ and $j=1+t_i^{\mathsf{next}}/P_i$, including skipped releases. The clock used by \textsc{Now} starts at zero. Metric collection is omitted from both algorithms.

\subsection{Earliest Deadline First}
\label{EDF_subsection}
Unlike SuperLoop, Earliest Deadline First (EDF) selects jobs according
to their absolute deadlines.

We first consider preemptive EDF with immediate preemption. At each scheduling decision, the ready job with the earliest absolute deadline $r_{i,j}+D_i$ is selected (Section~\ref{sec:system_model}). If a newly released job has an earlier absolute deadline than the running job, it preempts the running job.

Fig.~\ref{fig:edf_schedule} illustrates EDF scheduling for the task set in Table~\ref{tab:superloop_tasks}.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{edf_execution_schedule.png}
	\caption{EDF schedule for Table~\ref{tab:superloop_tasks}. Arrows mark releases and hatching indicates waiting.}
	\label{fig:edf_schedule}
\end{figure*}

At $t=20$, $J_{2,2}$ is released with $r_{2,2}+D_2=40<r_{1,1}+D_1=50$ and preempts $J_{1,1}$. After $J_{2,2}$ completes at $t=30$, $J_{1,1}$ resumes and completes at $t=40$.

Chunked EDF is used as a simplified prototype implementation, avoiding the additional complexity of full task-context switching during initial evaluation. Synthetic jobs execute in chunks of maximum duration $\Delta$, with job selection repeated after each chunk. An earlier-deadline job released during a chunk may therefore wait until the chunk completes. Sensor jobs use separate staged handlers.

For illustration, Fig.~\ref{fig:chunked_edf} uses $\Delta=4$ ms. Job $J_{2,2}$ is released at $t=20$ ms and preempts $J_{1,1}$ at the next chunk boundary $t=22$ ms. Thus, the preemption delay is $\delta=2$ ms. The final chunk of $J_{2,2}$ lasts $2$ ms, since the preceding two chunks account for $8$ ms of its execution time $C_2=10$ ms.

\begin{figure*}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[trim=0 40bp 0 0,clip,width=0.70\textwidth]{chunked_edf_execution_schedule.png}
	\caption{Chunk-bounded preemption in the EDF implementation.}
	\label{fig:chunked_edf}
\end{figure*}

Ignoring scheduling overhead, the delay until the next chunk boundary satisfies $0 \leq \delta \leq \Delta$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:chunked_edf} summarizes chunked EDF for synthetic jobs, assuming first releases at $t=0$. The set $\mathcal{R}$ contains active jobs, meaning jobs admitted for execution but not yet completed, including preempted jobs. Initially, $\mathcal{R}=\emptyset$, so no job is active. Equal deadlines are resolved by a fixed task order, the three-task experiments use $\tau_2,\tau_1,\tau_3$. \textsc{ExecuteChunk} executes at most $\Delta$ of the selected job's remaining execution and preserves its progress.

The auxiliary operations are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textsc{ReleaseJob}: creates and returns the job as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:superloop}, sets its absolute deadline to $r_{i,j}+D_i$, and additionally adds it to $\mathcal{R}$.
  \item \textsc{CompleteJob}: advances $t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$ using the skip rule in Algorithm~\ref{alg:superloop} and removes the completed job from $\mathcal{R}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
	\caption{Chunked EDF Scheduler}
	\label{alg:chunked_edf}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]

		\Function{ChunkedEDF}{$\mathcal{T},\Delta$}
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n$
		\Comment{Initial releases}

		\While{true}

		\State $t^{\mathsf{cur}} \gets \Call{Now}{}$

		\ForAll{$\tau_i \in \mathcal{T}$}
		\Comment{Check releases}
		\If{$t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \leq t^{\mathsf{cur}}$ and $\tau_i$ has no active job}
		\State \Call{ReleaseJob}{$\tau_i,t_i^{\mathsf{next}}$}
		\Comment{Add to $\mathcal{R}$}
		\EndIf
		\EndFor

		\If{$\mathcal{R} \neq \emptyset$}
		\Comment{Pending work}

		\State $J_{i,j} \gets
		\Call{SelectEDF}{\mathcal{R}}$
		\Comment{Earliest deadline}

		\State \Call{ExecuteChunk}{$J_{i,j},\Delta$}
		\Comment{At most $\Delta$}

		\If{\Call{Completed}{$J_{i,j}$}}
		\State $f_{i,j} \gets \Call{Now}{}$
		\State \Call{CompleteJob}{$J_{i,j},f_{i,j}$}
		\Comment{Skip rule}
		\EndIf

		\EndIf

		\EndWhile

		\EndFunction
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\subsection{Experimental setup}
Experiments run on the NUCLEO-F767ZI at 96 MHz. Physical sensors are disabled in these experiments. Each synthetic job executes a timed busy-wait workload, the scheduler also uses busy polling. Timing is obtained from the DWT cycle counter, and UART reports are emitted after measurement stops.

The results below use three tasks with $(P_1,P_2,P_3)=(10,50,200)$ ms and $D_i=P_i$. Table~\ref{tab:experimental_workloads} lists the configured synthetic workload durations, which are nominal budgets rather than measured worst-case bounds. Thus, $U_{\mathcal{T}}$ denotes nominal workload utilization and excludes instrumentation and scheduling overhead. Both schedulers use the same task set, SuperLoop polls tasks in index order. For clarity, the illustrative schedules assume synchronous first releases at $t=0$, whereas the implementation releases each task for the first time one period after initialization, $r_{i,1}=t_0+P_i$.

Additional campaigns use two tasks and 100 s windows to examine task-set and window-length effects. Extended diagnostic statistics are disabled in all these campaigns.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
Let $N_i$, $M_i$, and $S_i$ be the numbers of completed jobs, completed jobs that missed their deadlines, and skipped releases of $\tau_i$ in a run. Let $W$ be the requested measurement-window duration. We report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Deadline-miss ratio: $100M_i/N_i$ percent, defined for $N_i>0$.
  \item Skipped-release ratio: $100S_i/(W/P_i)$ percent, normalized by the nominal number of releases in the requested window.
  \item Average response time: the mean of $f_{i,j}-r_{i,j}$ over completed jobs. The maximum observed response time is also recorded.
  \item Selection overhead: measured time spent selecting jobs in iterations that execute work, divided by the actual measurement duration, in percent. This excludes uninstrumented scheduler work and is not total processor utilization.
\end{itemize}

Measurement stops at the first loop boundary after $W$, its actual duration may therefore be slightly longer. Released but unfinished tracked jobs and their overdue status are recorded separately and excluded from completion-based metrics. Subsequent releases awaiting skip accounting are not included in this pending-job count. Plot points are arithmetic means of the corresponding per-run metrics over three runs, not pooled averages over all jobs.

Its average response time under chunked EDF increases with utilization as jobs with earlier absolute deadlines execute first. At nominal $U_{\mathcal{T}}=100\%$, no capacity is reserved for scheduling and execution overhead, deadline misses and skipped releases are observed for $\tau_3$. The lower response time of $\tau_3$ under SuperLoop occurs alongside skipped releases of $\tau_1$, so it does not imply better service for the entire task set.
