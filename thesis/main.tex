\section{Introduction}
% 
\todo{Intro: Real time systems, STM32, What this paper examines? example: AB 2015 article}

STM32 is a family of microcontrollers developed by STMicroelectronics for embedded applications. These devices integrate a processor, memory, and peripheral interfaces on a single chip~\cite{STM32F767Datasheet}.

STM32-based devices support applications such as environmental monitoring~\cite{STM32EnvironmentMonitoring} and equipment condition monitoring~\cite{STM32ConditionMonitoring}. In these applications maintaining responsiveness as the workload increases is a key challenge.

The paper examines how the choice of scheduling approach affects responsiveness and processing cost on STM32. We compare two approaches: Superloop~\cite{KeilSuperLoop} and Earliest Deadline First (EDF)~\cite{LiuLayland1973}.

\subsection{Related works}
Previous studies have examined cyclic and cooperative scheduling approaches for embedded systems. Koopman \cite{Koopman2021BetterEmbedded} describes pure static scheduling, where tasks execute sequentially in a fixed order within a main loop. This approach is simple to implement and provides predictable execution. However, long-running tasks can delay subsequent tasks and cause deadline misses. Locke \cite{Locke1992SoftwareArchitecture} compares cyclic executives with fixed-priority executives. His analysis highlights that modifying predefined cyclic schedules can be difficult when task requirements change. Unlike predefined cyclic schedules, the Superloop considered in this work checks the readiness of periodic tasks in a fixed order and executes them when they are ready.

The limitations of cooperative scheduling have also been examined in practical applications. Jung et al. \cite{Jung2009HierarchicalPathPlanning} implemented a cooperative main loop for UAV control, where computationally intensive path-planning tasks were divided into smaller execution slices to meet the timing requirements of periodic control tasks. However, manually managing task execution times was complex, which motivated the use of a preemptive real-time kernel. Short \cite{Short2012TTCTTH} analyzed TTC and TTH schedulers and identified limitations in their scheduling overhead and schedulability. The proposed modifications reduced scheduling overhead and increased the proportion of schedulable task sets. Nevertheless, non-preemptive EDF achieved better schedulability in the evaluated scenarios.

Liu and Layland \cite{LiuLayland1973} analyzed Earliest Deadline First (EDF) scheduling, which selects the ready job with the earliest absolute deadline. They proved that, for independent periodic tasks with deadlines equal to their periods, preemptive EDF can schedule a task set on a single processor if and only if its total utilization does not exceed 100\%. However, this theoretical result assumes unrestricted preemption and does not explicitly model preemption overhead.

To reduce the costs of preemption, Bertogna and Baruah \cite{BertognaBaruah2010LimitedPreemption} proposed a limited-preemption EDF scheduler that delays preemptions when possible. Their simulations showed that, for task sets with ten tasks, the average number of preemptions was less than 20\% of that under fully preemptive EDF. Other studies have considered limiting preemption to specific points during task execution. Buttazzo et al. \cite{Buttazzo2013LimitedPreemptiveSurvey} reviewed several limited-preemption approaches, including fixed preemption points, where tasks are divided into non-preemptive chunks. Bertogna et al. \cite{Bertogna2010PreemptionPoints} proposed a method for placing these points to balance blocking time and preemption overhead. These studies demonstrate the importance of controlling when preemptions can occur, although their evaluations primarily rely on analytical models and simulations. The Chunked EDF considered in this work follows a related principle by selecting the earliest-deadline job after each non-preemptive chunk.

Experimental studies have also evaluated scheduling approaches on resource-constrained embedded platforms. Baynes et al. \cite{Baynes2003PerformanceEnergy} compared a minimal multirate scheduler with cooperative and preemptive RTOSs using a processor simulator. Their results showed no clear advantage of either cooperative or preemptive scheduling in timing accuracy, while the minimal scheduler had lower energy overhead. Marau et al. \cite{Marau2008FlexibleControl} implemented a minimal real-time kernel on a PIC18 microcontroller and showed that EDF scheduling overhead increased with the number of simultaneously released tasks. Oliveira and Lima \cite{OliveiraLima2023SchedulingEnergy} evaluated two EDF implementations on an STM32F407 running FreeRTOS. Their experiments showed that the implementation of the ready queue significantly affected scheduling overhead and deadline misses.

However, the reviewed experiments do not directly compare Superloop and Chunked EDF within the same bare-metal implementation. This work addresses this gap by evaluating both approaches on an STM32F767 under different processor utilization levels and, for Chunked EDF, different maximum chunk durations $\Delta$, measuring response times, deadline misses and skipped releases.


\subsection{Contribution}
First, we compare Superloop and chunked EDF for periodic tasks with implicit deadlines on an STM32F767 microcontroller. Then, we evaluate the effects of task-set utilization on response time, deadline misses, skipped releases, and measured task-selection overhead. Also, we investigate how maximum chunk duration affects these metrics for chunked EDF. Both scheduler implementations are publicly available.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system_model}

We use the periodic task model of Liu and Layland~\cite{LiuLayland1973}. We also adapt the task and release notation of Burmyakov and Bini~\cite{BurmyakovBini2026}. Table~\ref{tab:system_model} summarizes the notation.

A real-time system $\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_n\}$ consists of $n$ periodic tasks executed on one core ($m=1$). Each task $\tau_i=(C_i,D_i,P_i)$ is defined by the worst-case execution time $C_i$ of a job, a relative deadline $D_i$, and a period $P_i$. We consider implicit deadlines, $D_i=P_i$.

Let $J_{i,j}$ denote the $j$-th job of $\tau_i$, released at $r_{i,j}$. Consecutive jobs are released one period apart:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:constr_releases}
  r_{i,j+1}=r_{i,j}+P_i.
\end{equation}
Each job $J_{i,j}$ has an absolute deadline $d_{i,j}=r_{i,j}+D_i$. If completed, its finish time is denoted by $f_{i,j}$.

\begin{table}[H]
	\centering
	\caption{System model notation.}
	\label{tab:system_model}
	\small
	\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
	\begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
		\hline
		\textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
		\hline
		$\mathcal{T}, n$ & Task set and number of tasks \\
		$\tau_i$ & Task $i$ \\
		$C_i$ & Worst-case execution time of a job of $\tau_i$ \\
		$P_i$ & Task period \\
		$D_i=P_i$ & Relative deadline, equal to the period \\
		$J_{i,j}$ & The $j$-th job of task $\tau_i$ \\
		$r_{i,j}$ & Release time of job $J_{i,j}$ \\
		$f_{i,j}$ & Job completion time \\
		$d_{i,j}=r_{i,j}+D_i$ & Absolute deadline of job $J_{i,j}$ \\
		$m$ & Number of cores \\
		$U_i=C_i/P_i$ & Task utilization \\
		$U_{\mathcal{T}}=\sum_{\tau_i\in\mathcal{T}} U_i$ & Task-set utilization \\
		\hline
	\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
	\item A schedule specifies which job executes at each time, if any.
	\item Preemption interrupts a job before completion to execute another job. The interrupted job may resume later.
	\item A completed job $J_{i,j}$ misses its deadline if $f_{i,j}>d_{i,j}$.
	\item The response time of a completed job $J_{i,j}$ is $f_{i,j}-r_{i,j}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Superloop and EDF Schedulers}
\label{sec:schedulers}

We next describe the Superloop and chunked EDF schedulers used in our experiments. Fully preemptive EDF serves as a reference for comparison.

\subsection{Superloop}
\label{sec:superloop}

The Superloop scheduler has three key features:
%
\begin{itemize}
	\item Periodic tasks: each task can run only after its next release time.
	\item Fixed order: ready tasks run in the same order.
	\item Non-preemptive: each job runs until it finishes.
\end{itemize}

At the start of each loop iteration, the scheduler checks all tasks using the same current time. A task that becomes ready during execution is considered in the next iteration.

Similar approaches to periodic task scheduling in a main loop are described by Koopman~\cite{Koopman2015MultiRate} and Jones~\cite{Jones2024RTOSPart1}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:superloop} shows this process.

%
\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\caption{Superloop Scheduler}
	\label{alg:superloop}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Function{Superloop}{$\mathcal{T}$}
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n$
		\Comment{Initial releases}
		
		\While{true}
		\Comment{Infinite cycle}
		\State $t^{\mathsf{cur}} \gets \textproc{Now}()$
		\Comment{Get current system time}
		
		\For{$i \gets 1$ to $n$}
		\State $\mathit{ready}_i \gets (t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \leq t^{\mathsf{cur}})$
		\Comment{Save readiness}
		\EndFor
		
		\For{$i \gets 1$ to $n$}
		\If{$\mathit{ready}_i$}
		\State $J_{i,j} \gets
		(\tau_i,t_i^{\mathsf{next}})$
		\Comment{Keep release time}
		\State \Call{Execute}{$J_{i,j}$}
		\State $f_{i,j} \gets \textproc{Now}()$
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}} \gets
		t_i^{\mathsf{next}}+P_i$
		\Comment{Advance, then skip}
		\State \textbf{while}
		$t_i^{\mathsf{next}}<f_{i,j}$
		\textbf{do}
		$t_i^{\mathsf{next}}\gets
		t_i^{\mathsf{next}}+P_i$
		\EndIf
		\EndFor
		\EndWhile
		\EndFunction
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}




Fig.~\ref{fig:scheduler_schedules}\subref{fig:superloop_schedule} illustrates the Superloop schedule for tasks $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$. Both tasks release their first jobs at $t=0$. Since $\tau_1$ comes first in the polling order, $J_{1,1}$ executes until $t=18$ ms. Job $J_{2,1}$ then starts and completes at $f_{2,1}=28$ ms, missing its deadline $d_{2,1}=20$ ms.

The release at $r_{2,2}=20$ ms occurs while $J_{2,1}$ is still running. Once this job completes, the scheduler counts that release as skipped and advances the next release time to $r_{2,3}=40$ ms. Polling continues with no ready job until then.

\subsection{Earliest Deadline First}
\label{EDF_subsection}

In contrast, fully preemptive EDF selects the ready job with the earliest absolute deadline. A newly released job preempts the running job if its deadline is earlier.

Fig.~\ref{fig:scheduler_schedules}\subref{fig:edf_schedule} shows the EDF schedule for the same task set. At $t=0$, $J_{2,1}$ has the earlier deadline and executes until $t=10$ ms. Job $J_{1,1}$ then starts, but is preempted at $r_{2,2}=20$ ms because the new job has a deadline $d_{2,2}=40$ ms, earlier than $d_{1,1}=50$ ms. Job $J_{2,2}$ executes until $t=30$ ms, after which $J_{1,1}$ resumes and completes at $t=38$ ms. The next job of $\tau_2$ executes from $t=40$ to $t=50$ ms. Thus, no deadline is missed and no release is skipped in this example.


\subsection{Chunked Earliest Deadline First}
\label{chunked_EDF_subsection}

Unlike fully preemptive EDF, our chunked EDF implementation allows switching between jobs only after an execution step finishes. Its key features are:
%
\begin{itemize}
	\item Earliest deadline first: the ready job with the earliest absolute deadline is selected.
	\item Chunked execution: jobs run in non-preemptive steps. For computational workloads, each chunk lasts at most $\Delta$ ms.
	\item Repeated selection: the scheduler selects a job after each execution step.
\end{itemize}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:chunked_edf} shows this process. The Boolean flag $a_i$ indicates whether task $\tau_i$ has an unfinished job. At each iteration, $\mathcal{R}$ contains the indices of runnable tasks. These include tasks with due releases and active jobs that can resume. Jobs waiting for an external event are excluded.

For an active job, $d_i=r_i+D_i$, where $r_i$ is its saved release time. Otherwise, $d_i=t_i^{\mathsf{next}}+D_i$.

%
\begin{algorithm}[H]
	\caption{Chunked EDF Scheduler}
	\label{alg:chunked_edf}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Function{ChunkedEDF}{$\mathcal{T},\Delta$}
		\State $t_i^{\mathsf{next}}\gets 0,\quad
		\mathit{hasActiveJob}_i\gets\mathsf{false},\quad i=1,\ldots,n$
		
		\While{true}
		\Comment{Infinite cycle}
		\State $t^{\mathsf{cur}}\gets\textproc{Now}()$
		\Comment{Get current system time}
		\State $\mathcal{R}\gets
		\{i\mid\Call{Ready}{i,t^{\mathsf{cur}}}\}$
		\Comment{Ready task indices}
		
		\If{$\mathcal{R}\neq\emptyset$}
		\State $i^\star\gets
		\arg\min_{i\in\mathcal{R}}d_i$
		\Comment{Earliest deadline}
		
		\If{$\neg \mathit{hasActiveJob}_{i^\star}$}
		\State $r_{i^\star}\gets t_{i^\star}^{\mathsf{next}}$;
		\State $\mathit{hasActiveJob}_{i^\star}\gets\mathsf{true}$
		\Comment{Save release}
		\EndIf
		
		\State \Call{ExecuteStep}{$i^\star,\Delta$}
		\Comment{At most $\Delta$}
		
		\If{\Call{Completed}{$i^\star$}}
		\State $f\gets\textproc{Now}()$
		\State $t_{i^\star}^{\mathsf{next}}
		\gets t_{i^\star}^{\mathsf{next}}
		+P_{i^\star}$
		\Comment{Advance, then skip}
		\State \textbf{while}
		$t_{i^\star}^{\mathsf{next}}<f$
		\textbf{do}
		$t_{i^\star}^{\mathsf{next}}
		\gets t_{i^\star}^{\mathsf{next}}
		+P_{i^\star}$
		\State $\mathit{hasActiveJob}_{i^\star}\gets\mathsf{false}$
		\EndIf
		\EndIf
		\EndWhile
		\EndFunction
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Fig.~\ref{fig:scheduler_schedules}\subref{fig:chunked_edf} shows the same task set with $\Delta=4$ ms. At $r_{2,2}=20$ ms, job $J_{1,1}$ is still executing a chunk. This chunk ends at $t=22$ ms. Job $J_{2,2}$ then starts with a preemption delay of $\delta=2$ ms. It executes in chunks of 4, 4, and 2 ms and completes at $t=32$ ms. The shorter final chunk reflects the remaining work. Job $J_{1,1}$ then resumes and completes at $t=38$ ms.
